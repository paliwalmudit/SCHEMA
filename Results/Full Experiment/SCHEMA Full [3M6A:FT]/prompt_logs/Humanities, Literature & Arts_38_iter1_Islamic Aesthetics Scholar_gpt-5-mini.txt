In Islamic thought, comparativeness appears both as a hermeneutic device (Qur’anic metaphors and parables) and as metaphysical grammar (hierarchies of being, degrees of manifestation of the Divine). The theoretical framework I propose links three interlocking registers.

1) Theophanic-cosmological register: Beauty is understood as divine self-disclosure (tajallī) manifest in graded multiplicity. Neoplatonic emanationism in classical kalam and falsafa (al-Farabi, Ibn Sīnā, Suhrawardī) stages being and beauty in hierarchical degrees; Sufi texts (Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī) treat comparative images as means to perceive levels of presence.

2) Ethical-aesthetic register: Aesthetic judgment is inseparable from moral value (ihsān, taqwa). Al-Ghazālī and later moral aesthetics tie good form to good action; the “best” and “more beautiful” are normative categories rather than neutral taste. Jurisprudential debates (on ornament, images, architecture) show how comparison mediates permitted/forbidden.

3) Rhetorical-literary register: The Qur’an and Arabic rhetorical tradition (balāgha, al-jahiz) use similitude (mithāl, amthāl), parable, and contrast to shape perception; poetry and adab cultures cultivate comparative tropes to refine taste and social hierarchy.
