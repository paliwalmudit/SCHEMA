  "Related Work": "Prior research links financial literacy to better financial outcomes and identifies psychological mechanisms such as financial self-efficacy, budgeting adherence, and self-control. Many studies are cross-sectional or small-scale, limiting causal claims about mediation. Existing mediation work often uses simple regression steps or single-method measurement and rarely models mixed indicator outcomes (subjective scales plus objective behaviors) in a unified latent construct. Power and design-effect adjustments for clustered samples and mediation tests are uncommon. Transaction-linkage studies face selection bias but typically lack robust inverse-probability or selection-model corrections. Studies also rarely pre-specify sensitivity analyses for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding or model spillovers in student social networks. These gaps reduce confidence in causal mediation, measurement precision, and generalizability.",
  "Abstract": "This study tests whether psychological mechanisms explain how financial literacy improves students' financial wellbeing. We integrate financial literacy training with Conservation of Resources theory and evaluate mediation by self-control, mental budgeting, and mental accounting using survey data from university students. We derive a single latent wellbeing endpoint at six months that combines subjective scales and objective behavior using a hybrid CFA–IRT measurement model (CFA = confirmatory factor analysis; IRT = item response theory). We estimate causal paths with partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) and complement with multilevel Monte Carlo simulations to assess power under clustering (ICC = intraclass correlation coefficient). Main contributions are: (1) preregistered causal mediation tests with sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding, (2) a hybrid measurement of financial wellbeing that integrates mixed indicators, and (3) transparent power and selection-adjustment procedures for optional transaction linkage. Expected outcomes are clear estimates of direct and indirect effects and practical guidance on the role of psychological resources in boosting student financial wellbeing.",
  "Experiments": "Datasets: primary survey of N=781 Indonesian university students (baseline, immediate post, 3-month, 6-month), optional transaction-linked subset (expected consent scenarios: 50%, 40%, 30%), and simulated datasets for Monte Carlo power analyses. Baselines: (A) direct-effect model (financial literacy → wellbeing), (B) mediation PLS-SEM (financial literacy → mediators → latent wellbeing), (C) covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) with the same structure, (D) multilevel Bayesian SEM when clustering is present. Measurement models: hybrid CFA–IRT for latent wellbeing (continuous subjective indicators via CFA; dichotomous/objective indicators via IRT). Evaluation metrics: standardized average treatment effect (ATE) on latent wellbeing, standardized indirect effects (a*b) with 95% bootstrap CIs, p-values, model fit indices (CFI, RMSEA, SRMR for CFA; SRMR and Q2 for PLS-SEM), proportion mediated, Monte Carlo power curves, and sensitivity metrics (VanderWeele mediation bounds, Imai sensitivity, E-values). Missing-data diagnostics: fraction missing, convergence of imputations, and tipping-point grids. Experimental setup: estimate main models on observed data with ITT principle (intention-to-treat); run mediator ablations removing one mediator at a time; compare models with and without transaction-derived objective indicators; bootstrap indirect effects with 5,000 draws; run Monte Carlo multilevel SEM simulations with 5,000–10,000 replications across ICCs 0.01–0.10, cluster sizes m ∈ {10,20,30}, and attrition rates {10%,20%,30%}. Bayesian robustness: run stan/brms multilevel SEM with 4,000 iterations, 1,000 warmup, and report R̂ and effective sample sizes. Pre-specified comparisons: PLS-SEM vs CB-SEM, models with vs without transaction linkage, full mediation vs single-mediator ablations.",
  "Risk Factors and Limitations": "Self-report bias: mediators and some outcomes rely on self-reports and may be biased. Low transaction-link consent: linked data under 40% will reduce objective-behavior analyses and may induce selection bias. Unmeasured confounding: mediation identification relies on sequential ignorability (no unmeasured mediator–outcome confounders); violation will bias indirect estimates. Missing data and MNAR (missing not at random): attrition or selective nonresponse may invalidate MAR-based methods. Measurement noninvariance: latent constructs may differ across language, sex, or SES groups, limiting comparability. Spillover/interference: peer effects in campuses may violate SUTVA and bias ATEs if not modeled. Power limits under clustering: high ICC or small cluster counts inflate required N and may reduce power. Generalizability: sample from Indonesian universities may not generalize to other countries or age groups. Computational limits: Monte Carlo and Bayesian multilevel SEM are compute-intensive and may be slow for large simulation grids."
