Theoretical framing
Fuel-subsidy reform should be understood as a policy choice at the intersection of public finance, distributive politics, elite bargaining, and state legitimacy. Subsidy removal simultaneously creates fiscal space and redistributes rents; its political feasibility depends on elite coalitions, framing (discourse), organized interests, and compensatory mechanisms. Discourse matters because narratives (fiscal crisis, targeting inefficiency, patriotism) shape public perceptions, elite incentives, and coalition stability—thereby determining both the adoption and durability of reforms.

Key causal mechanisms to investigate
1. Fiscal mechanism: Subsidy cuts reduce recurrent fiscal outlays and free resources for alternative spending or debt reduction; the magnitude depends on price changes and pass-through to the budget.  
2. Distributional/welfare mechanism: Changes in real incomes (especially for poor and energy-intensive sectors) affect consumption, inflation, and poverty, mediating social unrest and political backlash.  
3. Compensatory mechanism: Design and delivery of compensatory transfers (cash, targeted subsidies) determine whether losses are mitigated and political costs reduced.  
4. Elite bargaining and patronage mechanism: Redistribution of rents alters patronage networks; elites who benefit from subsidy rents resist reforms unless compensated via new transfers, contracts, or spending.  
5. Discursive legitimation mechanism: Government narratives reframe winners/losers and responsibility, influencing public acceptance and elite cohesion.  
6. Policy durability mechanism: Institutional constraints, monitoring, and the alignment of technocratic agencies and political actors affect whether reforms persist or are reversed.

Testable hypotheses
H1: Larger measured fiscal savings from subsidy reduction are associated with increased public spending on infrastructure, health, or education within two fiscal years, conditional on political control of the executive.

H3: Regions or sectors with greater exposure to energy costs experience larger negative welfare shocks and higher protest incidence following subsidy removal, ceteris paribus.  
H4: Strong pro-reform discursive framing (emphasizing efficiency and targeting) predicts higher elite cohesion and reduced likelihood of policy reversal.  
H5: Political actors dependent on patronage-based rents are more likely to obstruct subsidy reform unless provided with alternative rent channels.

Measurement and identification suggestions
Combine discourse analysis (media, parliamentary transcripts, presidential speeches) with quantitative causal inference: event studies around announcements, difference-in-differences exploiting spatial heterogeneity in energy exposure, synthetic control for national-level reforms, and mediation analysis to test discourse → acceptability → durability pathways.
