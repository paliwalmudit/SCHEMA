Otolaryngology (ENT) Surgical Expert: "The surgical care pathway for adult obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) should be integrated seamlessly with the broader diagnostic and therapeutic framework outlined by sleep medicine teams to optimize patient outcomes. Surgical evaluation is indicated primarily for patients with moderate to severe OSA who have failed or are intolerant to first-line positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy, those with anatomical abnormalities amenable to surgical correction, or patients with favorable anatomy identified during a comprehensive upper airway assessment. Referral thresholds include persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, anatomical factors such as retrognathia, macroglossia, or multilevel airway collapse identified by nasoendoscopy with or without drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE), and obstructive patterns unsuitable for mandibular advancement devices (MAD).

Preoperative assessment must be thorough and multidisciplinary. It includes detailed airway anatomical evaluation using clinical examination and imaging modalities such as cephalometry or cone-beam CT when indicated. DISE is invaluable to characterize collapsibility patterns and guide surgical planning. Patient comorbidities, including cardiovascular status and BMI, should be assessed to determine surgical risk and candidacy. Sleep study data (AHI severity, positional dependency, oxygen desaturation index) help tailor intervention choice. Shared decision-making with patients regarding surgical options, risks, expected outcomes, and postoperative recovery is essential.

Surgical options comprise a spectrum from soft tissue procedures, such as uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP) and tonsillectomy, to upper airway stimulation via hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS), and skeletal surgeries like maxillomandibular advancement (MMA). UPPP is effective predominantly in patients with isolated oropharyngeal obstruction and tonsillar hypertrophy, offering AHI reductions around 40–60% but with variable long-term success and risk of postoperative velopharyngeal insufficiency. HNS, indicated under the STAR criteria (BMI <32 kg/m2, absence of complete concentric palatal collapse), shows promising mid-term outcomes with significant improvements in AHI and quality of life and lower adverse event rates but requires implant surgery and is costly. MMA is considered the most efficacious surgical intervention for moderate to severe OSA with skeletal abnormalities, achieving AHI reductions often exceeding 70%, and improvements in oxygenation and symptoms; however, it carries greater surgical morbidity and longer recovery. Comparative studies highlight that surgical choice should be individualized based on anatomy, patient preference, comorbidities, and prior treatment responses.

Postoperative follow-up combines clinical reassessment and objective sleep testing, ideally repeat polysomnography or HSAT at 3–6 months to quantify surgical efficacy. Symptomatology, adherence to adjunctive therapies (positional therapy, weight management), and potential complications such as dysphagia or speech changes should be monitored. Long-term surveillance is important as some patients may require adjunct procedures or transition to alternate therapies.

Coordination between surgeons and sleep medicine specialists is vital for holistic patient management. Multidisciplinary clinics improve case selection, perioperative optimization, and ensure continuity of care including PAP re-titration or alternative therapy initiation if surgery is insufficient. Integration of surgical pathways into the existing care framework supports personalized treatment plans and optimizes resource utilization.

Research gaps remain in predictive phenotyping for surgical outcomes, optimal timing for interventions, comparative effectiveness of different surgical modalities, and strategies to enhance patient selection and perioperative care. Prospective, multicenter studies linking collapsibility patterns and physiological traits with surgical response would enable precision surgery. Furthermore, long-term outcome data comparing surgery versus advanced medical therapies in diverse phenotypes are needed. Enhanced collaboration between surgical and sleep medicine teams will facilitate these research endeavors and improve evidence-based care pathways in OSA management."

  - Quality controls: minimum usable data fraction per channel (e.g., > 90% of recording time with valid signals); artifact documentation; double-scoring or cross-check for a subset to ensure reliability.
- Home sleep apnea testing (HSAT)
  - Device requirements: validated Type II/III HSAT with at least nasal airflow (or surrogate), thoracic/abdominal effort, oximetry; optional body position sensor; time-synced clock; data transmission capability; clear patient instructions.
  - Recording standards: minimum data duration of 4 hours of total recording with acceptable signal across key channels; allow single-night testing with acceptance criteria defined a priori; move to PSG if HSAT data are inconclusive or if red flags are present.
  - Scoring and interpretation: compute AHI from HSAT; document ODI, total recording time, sleep efficiency proxy if available; note limitations compared with PSG (e.g., absence of EEG-based sleep staging); record comorbid conditions that may affect accuracy.

Device selection and interpretation workflows
- Selection criteria
  - Use PSG for diagnostic uncertainty, suspected comorbid sleep disorders, cardiopulmonary disease, neuromuscular impairment, or when HSAT is negative but clinical suspicion remains high.
  - Use HSAT for uncomplicated adult OSA with high pretest probability and no major cardiopulmonary comorbidity; prefer devices with validated accuracy and robust data integrity, and ensure remote data retrieval capabilities.
- Interpretation workflow
  - Follow AASM/ERS scoring rules; implement double reading for a random subset to monitor inter-rater reliability.
  - Ensure data integrity checks before scoring (sensor failure alerts, time stamps, event markers).
  - Provide actionable reports: AHI/ODI, symptom correlation, comorbidity notes, recommended follow-up and therapeutic options, and adherence plan.

Implementation checklists for diagnostic services
- Pretest
  - Confirm eligibility, obtain consent, assess comorbidities, and provide device-use instructions; ensure BMI and anatomy considerations are documented for therapy planning.
- Data collection and QC
  - Verify sensor placement, calibration, impedance, and time synchronization; run a brief pretest as needed; document any interruptions or artifacts.
- Post-processing and reporting
  - Apply standardized scoring, perform quality checks, generate clinician-friendly reports with interpretive comments and treatment recommendations; archive raw data securely.
- Follow-up and adherence
  - Establish adherence monitoring (≥4 h/night on ≥70% of nights); schedule re-evaluation or repeat testing if symptoms persist or weight changes; initiate therapy optimization as indicated.

Research gaps and ideas
- Predictive phenotyping studies linking loop gain, arousal thresholds, collapsibility, and muscle tone to treatment response across modalities to enable personalized therapy.
- Pragmatic cardiovascular outcomes trials comparing CPAP, MAD, and weight-management interventions in distinct OSA phenotypes.
- Real-world implementation science: telemonitoring and digital-health interventions to improve CPAP adherence and outcomes; HSAT performance in AF, HF, and COPD populations with cost-effectiveness analyses.

Otolaryngology (ENT) Surgical Expert: "The surgical care pathway for adult obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) should be integrated seamlessly with the broader diagnostic and therapeutic framework outlined by sleep medicine teams to optimize patient outcomes. Surgical evaluation is indicated primarily for patients with moderate to severe OSA who have failed or are intolerant to first-line positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy, those with anatomical abnormalities amenable to surgical correction, or patients with favorable anatomy identified during a comprehensive upper airway assessment. Referral thresholds include persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, anatomical factors such as retrognathia, macroglossia, or multilevel airway collapse identified by nasoendoscopy with or without drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE), and obstructive patterns unsuitable for mandibular advancement devices (MAD).

Preoperative assessment must be thorough and multidisciplinary. It includes detailed airway anatomical evaluation using clinical examination and imaging modalities such as cephalometry or cone-beam CT when indicated. DISE is invaluable to characterize collapsibility patterns and guide surgical planning. Patient comorbidities, including cardiovascular status and BMI, should be assessed to determine surgical risk and candidacy. Sleep study data (AHI severity, positional dependency, oxygen desaturation index) help tailor intervention choice. Shared decision-making with patients regarding surgical options, risks, expected outcomes, and postoperative recovery is essential.

Surgical options comprise a spectrum from soft tissue procedures, such as uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP) and tonsillectomy, to upper airway stimulation via hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS), and skeletal surgeries like maxillomandibular advancement (MMA). UPPP is effective predominantly in patients with isolated oropharyngeal obstruction and tonsillar hypertrophy, offering AHI reductions around 40–60% but with variable long-term success and risk of postoperative velopharyngeal insufficiency. HNS, indicated under the STAR criteria (BMI <32 kg/m2, absence of complete concentric palatal collapse), shows promising mid-term outcomes with significant improvements in AHI and quality of life and lower adverse event rates but requires implant surgery and is costly. MMA is considered the most efficacious surgical intervention for moderate to severe OSA with skeletal abnormalities, achieving AHI reductions often exceeding 70%, and improvements in oxygenation and symptoms; however, it carries greater surgical morbidity and longer recovery. Comparative studies highlight that surgical choice should be individualized based on anatomy, patient preference, comorbidities, and prior treatment responses.

Postoperative follow-up combines clinical reassessment and objective sleep testing, ideally repeat polysomnography or HSAT at 3–6 months to quantify surgical efficacy. Symptomatology, adherence to adjunctive therapies (positional therapy, weight management), and potential complications such as dysphagia or speech changes should be monitored. Long-term surveillance is important as some patients may require adjunct procedures or transition to alternate therapies.

Coordination between surgeons and sleep medicine specialists is vital for holistic patient management. Multidisciplinary clinics improve case selection, perioperative optimization, and ensure continuity of care including PAP re-titration or alternative therapy initiation if surgery is insufficient. Integration of surgical pathways into the existing care framework supports personalized treatment plans and optimizes resource utilization.

Research gaps remain in predictive phenotyping for surgical outcomes, optimal timing for interventions, comparative effectiveness of different surgical modalities, and strategies to enhance patient selection and perioperative care. Prospective, multicenter studies linking collapsibility patterns and physiological traits with surgical response would enable precision surgery. Furthermore, long-term outcome data comparing surgery versus advanced medical therapies in diverse phenotypes are needed. Enhanced collaboration between surgical and sleep medicine teams will facilitate these research endeavors and improve evidence-based care pathways in OSA management."

Collectively, these contributions will ensure the care pathway not only meets rigorous scientific and operational standards but also directly addresses psychosocial adherence barriers, health literacy needs, and equity considerations central to patient experience. I am committed to delivering these toolkits, workflows, engagement protocols, and measurement frameworks aligned with the multidisciplinary timelines and ready for integration and validation in the upcoming pilot and multisite implementation phases. This approach fully complements the physiologic, surgical, and economic components to yield a truly patient-centered, equitable, and sustainable OSA care pathway.
