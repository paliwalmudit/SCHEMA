Governmentality & Critical Policy Theorist: "Proposed theoretical framework (summary)
Governmental rationalities (e.g., neoliberal human-capital, managerialist, welfare liberal) operate as ensembles of knowledges, techniques and moralities that problematise certain educational phenomena and normalise particular responses. Drawing on Foucault’s governmentality, Pierre Bourdieu (fields/habitus) and epistemic injustice theory, I propose a framework where policy rationalities produce regimes of visibility and invisibility (what counts as a problem, what counts as evidence), shape subjectivities (students, parents, teachers as accountable, deficient or entrepreneurial), and thereby generate processes of misrecognition: systematic distortions in how human and social conditions are named, measured and governed.

Key conceptual moves
- Problematisation: Policies construct problems through classificatory acts (categorisation, labelling), narrative frames (deficit, risk, economic urgency), and calculative devices (tests, indicators). These acts transform complex social conditions into governable objects (e.g., “low attainment”, “English as an Additional Language”) by isolating individual attributes from structural causes.
- Normalisation: Techniques (standards, inspections, funding formulas, performative targets) create normative benchmarks that render certain behaviours and capacities as legitimate or aberrant. Repeated practices of audit, commensuration and incentivisation make these norms appear natural and inevitable.
- Misrecognition mechanisms: Selective visibility (what is measured), commensuration (reducing heterogeneous capacities to single metrics), responsibilisation (shifting responsibility from structural actors to individuals), pathologisation (framing communities or languages as deficit), and depoliticisation (removing political-economic causes from discourse) together produce epistemic and moral misrecognition of lived conditions.

Operational mechanisms to analyse empirically
- Classification technologies: tests, codes, eligibility criteria that reify categories and exclude intersectional nuance.
- Evidence regimes: what counts as valid evidence (randomised trials, attainment data) and which knowledges are marginalised (lived experience, community knowledge).
- Incentive architectures: funding, sanctions and rewards that steer practice toward measurable outcomes.
- Discourse coalitions and expertisation: networks that legitimate certain knowledge claims and marginalise alternative framings.
- Surveillance and reporting infrastructures: data flows that produce subjects through repeated measurement.
