Building on the comprehensive interdisciplinary foundation and prioritized pilot roadmap, I propose a focused behavioral science research agenda tailored to ensure that true price signals are both cognitively accessible and behaviorally effective across diverse consumer segments. First, I recommend conducting mixed-method formative research—including in-depth qualitative interviews and ethnographic observation—stratified by income, environmental attitudes, and cognitive styles to uncover how consumers interpret different true-price presentation formats (aggregated scores, multi-attribute breakdowns, comparative rankings). This will elucidate complexity thresholds and heuristics consumers use, guiding message framing to balance informativeness with simplicity and prevent cognitive overload.

Second, embedding randomized controlled experiments within the three main pilots (grocery labeling, electricity TOU pricing, congestion pricing) is essential to rigorously test specific nudges and choice architectures. For grocery, experiments should compare simplified eco-score labels versus detailed footprints, combined with social norm messages and default product sorting by true price. For electricity, varying carbon price framings, peak-time salience cues, and commitment devices will allow assessment of framing effects on demand response. For congestion pricing, income-based rebates paired with fairness and transparency messaging should be experimentally evaluated for acceptability and behavioral impact. These experiments must track behavioral shifts alongside intermediate outcomes such as cognitive load (via brief validated metrics), trust in labels, fairness perceptions, and message fatigue over time.

Third, message design should follow core behavioral principles: prioritize actionable, positively framed information (e.g., emissions avoided, cost savings), minimize jargon, and use relatable analogies. Transparency about uncertainty and data provenance should be conveyed succinctly through intuitive symbols and trusted third-party endorsements to build confidence without overwhelming users. Designing layered information formats—enabling high-engagement consumers to access detailed data while presenting simple heuristics to others—can enhance broad accessibility.

Fourth, integrating behavioral metrics into the pilots’ evaluation framework is critical. Longitudinal panel surveys combined with digital trace data can reveal not only average treatment effects on purchasing or consumption but also persistence, spillover effects, and heterogeneity in response. Pre-specifying validated survey instruments measuring comprehension, trust, motivation, and cognitive burden will produce replicable, comparable data across pilots. Behavioral segmentation analyses will identify vulnerable groups and inform targeted messaging adaptations to promote equity.

Finally, close collaboration with mechanism design and data teams is vital to embed behavioral insights into incentive-compatible pricing menus and revenue recycling schemes. For example, behavioral segmentation can inform nonlinear menu design and framing of post-purchase rebates to optimize self-selection and avoid regressivity. Coordinating behavioral messaging with privacy and legal compliance protocols will further enhance consumer trust and uptake.

In sum, advancing true pricing requires a rigorous behavioral research pillar that ensures environmental externality signals are both scientifically credible and psychologically compelling. By systematically unpacking consumer cognition, testing choice architectures in situ, designing user-centered messages, and integrating robust behavioral measurement and segmentation, we can substantially increase the likelihood that true price interventions yield sustained, equitable real-world behavior change in the pilots and beyond. I recommend initiating a dedicated behavioral workstream with clear milestones aligned to the overall project timeline to operationalize these principles in tandem with economic, data, and lifecycle teams.
