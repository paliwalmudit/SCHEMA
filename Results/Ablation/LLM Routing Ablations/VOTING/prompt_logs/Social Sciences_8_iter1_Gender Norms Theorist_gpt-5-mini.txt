Summary and conceptual framing:
Unpaid elderly care in Thailand is embedded in gendered cultural meanings (filial duty, gratitude/bun khun, Buddhist merit-making) that shape expectations about altruistic behavior. Building on social norms theory, distinguish (1) injunctive norms (what people think ought to be done), (2) descriptive norms (what people observe others doing), and (3) internalized gender-role norms (identity-based expectations). Altruism norms (valuing self-sacrifice for kin) may operate both as an injunctive rule and as an internalized motive, linking norms to actual caregiving behavior.

Key theoretical pathways:
- Direct normative pressure: community injunctive norms (e.g., “daughters should care”) increase probability and intensity of care by women via social approval/sanctions.
- Observational reinforcement: descriptive norms (seeing mainly women provide care) sustain gendered division of labor.
- Internalization/identity: altruism norms become part of gender identity (moral duty, bun khun), leading to voluntary provision even when costly.
- Moderation/constraint: socioeconomic resources, co-residence, migration of adult children, formal care availability, and religiosity moderate normative effects.
- Potential backlash: strong altruism norms can increase subjective burden and reduce labor-market participation for caregivers.

Measurement suggestions (quantitative):
- Injunctive norms scale (3–5 Likert items): e.g., “People in my community believe daughters should personally care for elderly parents,” “It is expected that sons contribute financially but not provide daily care.”
- Descriptive norms scale (3–5 items): e.g., “Most families I know have daughters doing daily caregiving,” “Men in my community rarely do personal care tasks.”
- Altruism/internalization scale (4–6 items): e.g., “I feel it is my moral duty to sacrifice for elderly family members,” items referencing bun khun/merit-making to capture Thai cultural framing.
- Perceived sanctions/rewards: items on pride, shame, community approval, gossip.
- Care outcome measures: hours/week of care, types of tasks (ADL, IADL, emotional support), caregiver burden (Zarit or adapted), labor market adjustments.
- Covariates: co-residence, number/sex of siblings, socioeconomic status, urban/rural, migration of children, health needs of elder, religiosity.

Survey design refinements:
- Use vignettes varying caregiver gender, relationship, and cost to elicit normative judgment differences.
- Include indirect techniques (list experiments, randomized response) to reduce social desirability when measuring prescriptive beliefs.
- Stratify sampling by region and urban/rural to capture cultural heterogeneity.

Qualitative probes:
- Life-history interviews about care decisions, use of moral language (bun khun, merit), family bargaining among siblings, community reactions to nonconforming caregivers, intersections with paid work.
- Focus groups by gender and generation to surface community injunctive norms and sanctions.
- Compare narratives across socioeconomic strata and migrant vs. non-migrant families.

1) Stronger injunctive norms that women should be altruistic predict higher likelihood and greater intensity of unpaid elderly care by daughters, controlling for need and resources.
2) Descriptive norms mediating gendered care: where observed male caregiving is rare, care is more concentrated in women.
3) Internalized altruism mediates the relationship between norms and caregiving decisions; mediation is stronger among older cohorts and more religious respondents.
4) Socioeconomic resources and labor-market attachment moderate normative influence: norms predict caregiving more strongly where formal care is scarce and employment constraints are lower.
