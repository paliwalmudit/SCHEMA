Participants (parents, students, teachers, principal) receive either a development-framed policy vignette (emphasizing English as necessary for individual/social development) or a neutral vignette. Quantitative measures capture self-blame, willingness-to-pay for private tuition, and perceived linguistic capital; qualitative data are transcribed Bangla-first with Jeffersonian notation, coded thematically, and analysed for micro-interactional cues. Main contributions: (1) a causal test of policy messaging effects on low-SES family decisions; (2) an integrated micro-to-macro analytic workflow linking utterance-level affect and interaction to policy texts and theory (Bourdieu, Foucault, Ahmed); (3) reproducible protocols for transcription, translation, coding and reliability. Expected outcome: evidence on whether development-framed policy language drives self-responsibilisation and market responses in low-SES communities, informing equitable language policy design.",

Jeffersonian notation: a detailed way to mark talk features (pauses, overlaps). 7) Coding & analysis: develop codebook with deductive families (Bourdieu: linguistic capital, misrecognition; Foucault: audit, subjectification; Ahmed: affect, stickiness) and inductive codes. Unit of analysis primary: turn (speaker turn); annotate sub-utterance clauses where needed.   "Risk Factors and Limitations": "1) Causal inference limits: experiment tests short-term effects of messaging; long-term behavioural change (actual tuition enrolment) may not follow immediate responses. 2) Sample and generalisation: single-site or small multi-site samples limit external validity to other regions or urban/rural contexts. 3) Social desirability and demand effects: participants may answer to please interviewers or follow perceived expectations after vignettes, biasing measured outcomes. 4) Translation and annotation complexity: nuanced Bangla expressions, code-switching and prosody may be lost or inconsistently rendered in translation, affecting interpretive claims. 5) Measurement error: self-reported willingness-to-pay may not reflect real financial behaviour. 6) Interrater reliability: achieving high agreement on affective and theory-driven codes is challenging; low agreement reduces confidence in qualitative frequency comparisons. 7) Ethical and safeguarding risks: discussing stigma, shame or poverty can distress participants and requires robust referral pathways. 8) Data security and re-identification: small community data risks re-identification even after anonymisation, limiting what can be published. 9) Resource constraints: transcription with Jeffersonian detail and back-translation is time-consuming and costly, which may reduce sample sizes. 10) Analytical complexity: integrating micro-interactional detail with experimental statistics demands interdisciplinary skills; misalignment between qualitative and quantitative findings may complicate interpretation."
