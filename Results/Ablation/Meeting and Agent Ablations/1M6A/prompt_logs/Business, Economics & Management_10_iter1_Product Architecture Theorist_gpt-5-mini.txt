Problem summary: Quantifying modularity effects for modular product families remains difficult because "modularity" is multi-dimensional, context dependent, and often inferred from proxies (BOM, CAD, code) that capture only parts of architecture. To advance empirical and simulation work we need a clear conceptual framework, hypothesized mechanisms linking architecture to performance, and a mapping from theoretical constructs to measurable structural features.

Conceptual framework (core dimensions):
1. Decomposition and granularity: how a product is partitioned into modules (count, size distribution, functional scope).
2. Coupling and dependency structure: strength, directionality, and heterogeneity of inter-module dependencies.
3. Interface specification and standardization: formality, stability, and completeness of interfaces that enable interchangeability.
4. Commonality vs. variability: proportion of shared modules across the family and the controlled loci of variation (configurable module sets).
5. Integrability and reconfiguration cost: effort to assemble, replace, or reconfigure modules across variants.
6. Information modularity and development concurrency: how work bundles map to modules enabling parallel development.
7. Evolution dynamics: change propagation, learning, and architectural drift over time.

Hypotheses to test:
H1: Interface standardization moderates the coupling–performance relationship: higher standardization reduces the negative effect of coupling on time-to-market and quality.
H2: Granularity exhibits an inverted-U: increasing module granularity improves variety flexibility up to a point beyond which integration and coordination costs dominate.
H3: Higher commonality reduces per-unit cost but decreases firm ability to meet diverse market requirements beyond a context-dependent threshold.
H4: Sparse, well-structured dependency graphs (low coupling density, low centrality concentration) increase parallelism and lower development lead times.
H5: Architectural heterogeneity (mixture of strong and weak couplings) reduces the realized benefits of modularization compared with uniformly modular architectures.

Operationalization: mapping constructs to measurable features
- Decomposition: module count, average module complexity (parts count, LOC, function points), module size variance.
- Coupling: DSM coupling density, number of inter-module links, weighted link strength, centrality measures (degree, betweenness), change propagation probability.
- Interface standardization: presence of formal interface docs, number of interface version changes/year, interface API stability index, percent of interface parameters standardized.
- Commonality/variability: commonality ratio (shared modules / total modules), variant-per-module distribution, configurability index.
- Integrability cost: average assembly time, number of compatibility exceptions, integration defect rate, rework hours per integration.
- Information modularity: alignment of organizational teams to modules, concurrency index (parallel workdays), cross-team communication frequency.
- Evolution: rate of module change, churn per module, dependency rewiring frequency.

Data sources and methods: DSM from CAD/BOM/software repos, interface specification audits, assembly time logs, change-sets and commit histories, supplier catalogs. For simulations capture design rules, DSM adjacency matrices, interface stability parameters, substitution costs, learning curves, and supply constraints.

Recommendation: Use multi-metric modularity profiles rather than single indices; model interactions (moderation, non-linearities) and dynamics; validate measures across hardware and software product families to build robust, generalizable quantification.
