Theoretical framework (concise)
- Core concepts: Jamāl (beauty) functions in Islamic thought not merely as sensory pleasure but as manifestation of divine attributes (al-jamīl, al-ḥasan). Beauty is therefore aesthetic, moral, and ontological simultaneously: it signals right form, harmony, and spiritual grace.

- Comparativeness as practice: Comparative evaluation (ranking, analogizing, juxtaposing) operates within limits set by theological norms—tawḥīd (unity), adab (ethical conduct), and anti-pride injunctions—so comparison is often framed as discerning degrees of participation in divine beauty rather than asserting absolute superiority.
- Intellectual resources: Classical philosophers (al-Fārābī, Avicenna) supply formal aesthetics of form and proportion; Sufi masters (al-Ghazālī, Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī, al-Niffarī, Mulla Ṣadrā) provide experiential vocabularies (dhawq, ishq, tajriba) that shape comparative language; juristic and ethical texts regulate comparative conduct (e.g., admonitions against riyaʾ and ghīra).
