Theoretical framing
Social capital should be framed as relational resources embedded in networks that operate through informational, normative, and instrumental mechanisms. Distinguish bonding capital (strong, dense ties that provide emotional and practical support but limited novel information) from bridging capital (weaker, more heterogeneous ties that provide novel information, referrals, and access to institutional gateways). Digital social capital reflects how networked resources are accessed and mobilized through digital platforms and is mediated by digital literacy. Reproduction of inequality occurs when higher-SES families possess more bridging ties, higher-quality institutional linkages, and greater digital skills that convert ties into college access.

Operationalization and measurement
Use mixed measurement: name-generator network data, resource-generator items, and multi-item scales. Combine objective network counts with perceptual Likert items and digital literacy measures.

Recommended survey items (5-point Likert: 1=Strongly disagree to 5=Strongly agree) — Bonding social capital scale (example items):
- I can turn to family or close friends for emotional support about school decisions.
- Close family or friends help me complete school forms or applications.
- My family talks often about the importance of attending college.
- I regularly receive practical help from people in my immediate social circle when I need it.

Bridging social capital scale:
- I know people outside my immediate community who can provide advice about college or careers.
- I have acquaintances who work at or attended colleges or universities.
- I can get referrals from people I know to internships, college staff, or academic programs.
- I have contacts who can introduce me to professional or academic opportunities.

Resource/position-generator items (Yes/No):
- Do you personally know anyone who can help with college application essays?
- Do you know anyone who can arrange a campus visit or introduction to a college staff member?
- Do you know anyone who can help you find an internship relevant to college admission?

Digital social capital and digital literacy items:
- I use online platforms to find information about colleges or scholarships.
- I can evaluate and compare college resources I find online (digital literacy).
- I have contacts through social media or professional networks who provide college-related leads.

Objective network measures:
- Number of non-family alters nominated.
- Proportion of alters with college degrees.
- Presence of a mentor or counselor contact (binary).
- Frequency of contact with college-knowledgeable ties.

Testable hypotheses
1. Higher parental SES predicts greater bridging social capital (more non-family, college-educated alters), which increases probability of college application and enrollment.
2. Bridging social capital has a stronger positive association with college access than bonding social capital, net of SES and academic achievement.
3. Digital literacy moderates the effect of bridging ties: students with higher digital literacy convert online ties into college access more effectively.
4. Social capital partially mediates the relationship between SES and college access, accounting for a substantive portion of the SES effect.
5. For low-SES students, high levels of bridging social capital or institutional ties (mentors, counselors) can mitigate but not fully eliminate SES gaps in access.
