Corporate Finance Theorist: "Model sketch. Consider a continuum of identical banks that choose leverage L (debt-to-assets) or equivalently a capital ratio k = 1−L to maximize shareholder value. Each bank finances assets A with equity E and deposits D = L A. Asset returns are risky: realized asset value X = A·(1+ε) with ε drawn from a distribution F. Default occurs if X < D. Shareholders receive residual max{X−D,0}. Deposits are insured implicitly, creating a subsidy τ per unit of deposit (captures lower funding costs or bailout expectation). Corporate tax rate t creates a tax shield on interest payments. Distress imposes direct deadweight cost c when default happens and external social cost B (systemic spillovers, lost intermediation). Risk-shifting: shareholders can choose risk intensity r ∈ [0,1] that raises both expected return and variance; higher leverage amplifies incentive to increase r, modeled by r(L) increasing in L.

Private objective and first-order condition. Shareholder value V(L) = E[max{X−D,0}] + τ D + t·InterestShield(L) − cost of monitoring/agency. The FOC for an interior optimum equates marginal private benefit of additional leverage to marginal private expected cost:
MB_priv ≡ ∂/∂L [τ D + t·InterestShield(L) + upside leverage gain] = MC_priv ≡ p'(L)·E[cash shortfall + deadweight c given default] + ∂(risk‑shifting benefit)/∂L,
or in reduced form
t + τ + ψ(L) = p'(L)·(c + E_loss) ,
where p(L)=Pr(default) and ψ(L) captures marginal shareholder upside from higher upside volatility and risk‑shifting.

Social planner solves max SocialSurplus(L) = E[social payoff] − bailout + tax revenue − intermediation losses − systemic cost B·p(L). The social FOC replaces τ with 0 and adds B to the marginal cost:
t + 0 + ψ_social(L) = p'(L)·(c + E_loss + B).
Thus private leverage L_priv exceeds social optimum L_social when τ>0 or when private ψ overweights upside relative to social welfare.

Comparative statics. Higher implicit subsidy τ or higher tax rate t raises L_priv; higher distress cost c or systemic externality B reduces L_social and narrows welfare gap; stronger market discipline (higher depositor sensitivity, steeper p'(L) via runs/monitoring) lowers equilibrium L; decreased risk-shifting (via governance) reduces ψ and thus lowers leverage.

Welfare measure. Welfare loss from private leverage equals ∫_{L_social}^{L_priv} [p'(L)·(c+B) − (t + τ + ψ(L))] dL, which aggregates excess default probability times social cost minus any private tax benefit.

Testable hypotheses and observable moments.
1) Cross-sectional: banks with greater implicit subsidy (TBTF status, higher deposit insurance coverage) exhibit higher leverage and lower capital ratios.
2) Tax policy: increases in corporate tax rates are associated with higher bank leverage controlling for risk.
3) Distress exposure: banks with higher expected bankruptcy costs (large intermediation roles) hold higher capital ratios.
4) Market discipline: stronger creditor monitoring (shorter deposit maturities, higher uninsured deposits) correlates with lower leverage and smaller gap to social optimum.
5) Risk-shifting: measures of incentive distortion (CEO pay sensitivity, limited liability proxies) predict greater volatility, higher default probability, and lower capital ratios.
6) Macro: higher systemic externality B (during crises) raises socially optimal capital; regulatory capital increases should correspond to reductions in crisis frequency and bailouts.

Policy implications. Optimal bank capital regulation should correct implicit subsidies (deposit insurance pricing), limit tax advantages, and align shareholder incentives (governance/remuneration) while setting capital buffers that internalize systemic externalities and reduce risk‑shifting."
