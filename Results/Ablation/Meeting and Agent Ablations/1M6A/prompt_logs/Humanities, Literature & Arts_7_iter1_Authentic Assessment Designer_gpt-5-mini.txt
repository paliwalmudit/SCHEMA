Thank you — I will continue and improve. Below I propose concrete research-focused assessment designs, rubric structures, instructor guidance, and pilot methods aimed at revealing true student understanding and resisting AI-enabled shortcuts.

Key research gaps to address
- Most tasks assess final product only; process evidence is scarce.  
- AI can generate plausible answers for decontextualized prompts; personalized context and iterative work are understudied.  
- Instructor-facing rubrics rarely prioritize reasoning traces, source attribution, and interaction responsiveness.

Robust authentic task types (online-ready)
1. Staged problem with mandatory process artifacts: students submit initial plan, timed progress recordings (screen/audio), and final artifact. Tasks require choices (e.g., method selection) justified in each stage.  
2. Localized transfer project: apply course concepts to a student-specific, verifiable local context (e.g., analyze a campus dataset or neighborhood case). Personal context reduces generic AI reuse.  
3. Iterative design + critique: students produce a solution, receive instructor/peer feedback, revise, and write a rationale for changes referencing feedback and constraints.  
4. Constrained synthesis under live follow-up: submit written artifact then complete a short live or recorded oral follow-up that probes reasoning, asks for alternative solutions, and requires on-the-spot adjustments.  
5. Collaborative role-based deliverable with commit logs: teams produce role-specific contributions tracked via version control and reflective logs explaining decisions.

Mapping to learning outcomes
- Procedural fluency: assessed via screen recordings and stepwise artifacts showing method application.  
- Conceptual understanding: assessed by prompts requiring explanation of why chosen methods work and contrived failure cases.  
- Transfer and problem-solving: assessed through localized, novel-context tasks and revision rationales.  
- Communication and collaboration: assessed via peer-feedback quality, commit histories, and role reflections.

Rubric design principles and example items
- Analytic rubrics with separate strands: Process Evidence, Decision Rationale, Conceptual Accuracy, Appropriateness of Method, Revision Quality, Communication & Attribution.  
- Each strand has 3–4 levels (exceeds/meets/partial/insufficient) with explicit behavioral indicators (e.g., “provides stepwise reasoning with timestamps and justifies each decision using course concepts”).  
- Weight process and responsiveness heavily (40–60% combined) so polished AI-generated final products cannot score highly without process evidence.

Instructor-facing guidelines
- Require and grade process artifacts; use short live follow-ups focused on conceptual probing rather than factual recall.  
- Use rubrics during calibration sessions; include exemplar artifacts for each level.  
- Treat AI-detection tools as auxiliary; prioritize discrepancies between process artifacts and final submissions.  
- Design localized prompts and require explicit source attribution and reflection on any AI assistance.

Pilot study suggestions
- Randomly assign students to conditions (allowed-AI, no-AI, process-required) to measure effects on quality and detectability.  
- Collect metrics: rubric scores, inter-rater reliability, time-on-task, process artifact richness, AI-detection outputs, and follow-up oral performance.  
- Analyze which rubric strands best differentiate genuine understanding from AI-assisted shortcuts.
