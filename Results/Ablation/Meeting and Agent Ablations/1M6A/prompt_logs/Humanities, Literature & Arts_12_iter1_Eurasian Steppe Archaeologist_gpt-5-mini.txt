Summary and research priorities for reassessing the composite bow in Bronze–Iron Age Eurasia:

Synthesis and working hypotheses
- Composite bows are multi-material artefacts (wood core + horn + sinew + adhesives). Direct evidence is preserved only in cold/dry contexts (Pazyryk, some Xiongnu and Scythian burials). Indirect evidence (iconography, quiver/arrow form, bow fittings) is widespread. I propose two working hypotheses to test: (1) composite-bow technology emerged in the Late Bronze Age steppe (linked to chariot/horse innovations) and radiated both westward and eastward; (2) multiple, regionally distinct technological packages (differences in laminating recipes, species used, and adhesive chemistry) developed, so appearance and form varied spatially and temporally.

Priority sites and contexts for reanalysis (ranked)
1. Pazyryk kurgans (Altai) — exceptional organic preservation; Hermitage collections for direct material comparison.
2. Arzhan and Aldy-Bel (Tuva / Altai region) and Berel (Kazakhstan) — elite horse burials with organic/wood remnants in state museums (Russian and Kazakh collections).
3. Sintashta-Petrovka and Andronovo horizon cemeteries — strong association with chariots/horse culture and potential early composite use; museums in Chelyabinsk, Orenburg, and Kazakhstan.
4. Tagar and later Scythian contexts (Minusinsk Basin) — link to Pazyryk and material variants.
5. Xiongnu and early Han frontier burials (Mongolia, northern China) — to trace eastward developments; National Museum of Mongolia, Institute of Archaeology.
6. Dry/arid tombs and caves across Central Asia (Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan) and peat bogs where organic fragments may be unrecognized in local collections.

- Create a targeted database of candidate objects (bow fragments, glued laminations, horn/sinew offcuts, bow fittings and residues).
