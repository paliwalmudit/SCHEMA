Summary framework and operationalization for individual and collective epistemic virtues in science

1. Core definitions
- Individual epistemic virtues: stable dispositions or skills that reliably promote acquisition, retention, and transmission of true, justified, and useful beliefs in scientific practice (e.g., intellectual humility, epistemic conscientiousness, curiosity, methodological rigor).
- Collective epistemic virtues: emergent properties of groups, institutions, or practices that enhance group-level knowledge outcomes (e.g., epistemic trust, transparency, shared norms for error correction, epistemic diversity and justice).

2. Reconciling philosophical accounts
- Integrate reliabilist and responsibilist strands by distinguishing competence (reliable cognitive processes/skills) from character-like dispositions (motivation to pursue epistemic goods). Treat social-epistemic accounts as higher-level constraints and enabling structures (communication norms, incentives) that instantiate or frustrate individual virtues.
- Operational taxonomy: cognitive skills (reasoning, statistical literacy), affective-motivational dispositions (humility, curiosity), behavioral practices (data sharing, preregistration), and institutional affordances (open peer review, reward structures).

3. Testable hypotheses (examples)
- H1: Higher individual intellectual humility predicts greater willingness to update beliefs in light of replication failures, mediated by openness to critique.
- H2: Teams scoring higher on collective transparency and epistemic diversity produce results with higher reproducibility rates, controlling for methods and resources.
- H3: Interventions that train deliberative norms (structured disagreement protocols) increase team error-detection rates and reduce confirmation bias in subsequent tasks.
- H4: The effect of individual methodological rigor on research quality is moderated by institutional incentives (publish-or-perish intensity): stronger incentives diminish the positive effect.

4. Measurement criteria and instruments
- Individual-level: validated Likert scales (intellectual humility, curiosity), situational judgment tests, behavioral tasks (information-seeking, update tasks), and open-science behaviors (preregistration, data/code sharing).
- Collective-level: aggregated individual scores (with within-group agreement indices), independent expert ratings of lab practices, archival indicators (replication rates, data availability, retraction rates), social-network metrics (communication density, centralization), and content analysis of lab protocols and decision logs.
- Psychometric standards: report internal consistency, test-retest reliability, convergent/divergent validity, and predictive validity against objective outcomes (replicability, citation corrections).

5. Research designs and analysis
- Use multilevel designs (individuals nested in teams/institutions), longitudinal and pre-post intervention RCTs where feasible, and natural experiments (policy changes). Analyze with multilevel SEM, cross-lagged panels, and mediation/moderation models to separate individual vs. collective effects.

6. Implementation guidance
- Prioritize mixed-method validation: combine self-report, behavioral, and archival metrics; ensure sampling across disciplines; document contextual incentives. Pre-register measures and analytic plans to model epistemic virtues without introducing perverse incentives.

This framework aims to make philosophical constructs empirically tractable and to guide interventions that strengthen both individual and collective epistemic functioning in science.
