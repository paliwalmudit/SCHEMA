Overview: I propose a mechanistic framework in which subjective effort arises from dynamic interactions among three functional nodes — dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)/midcingulate, anterior insula, and striatum — modulated by ascending neuromodulatory systems (dopamine, noradrenaline) and interoceptive feedback. This framework integrates control-allocation (expected value of control), interoceptive inference, and cost–benefit reinforcement learning to generate testable neural and behavioral hypotheses.

Core theoretical claims:
1. dACC computes a control signal by integrating expected reward, expected task demands, uncertainty, and opportunity costs; its activity and midfrontal theta index both predicted control allocation and subjective effort during anticipation and execution.
2. Anterior insula encodes interoceptive predictions and prediction errors about bodily states; its moment-to-moment coupling with dACC maps onto the felt quality of effort (subjective effort reports), independent of objective task demands.
3. Ventral/dorsal striatum encodes motivational vigor and anticipated reward; when required effort increases, striatal valuation signals are down-weighted and their coupling with dACC predicts effort avoidance and discounting.
4. Dopamine modulates the cost–benefit weighting (changing willingness to invest effort and vigor); noradrenergic/LC activity (indexed by pupil dilation) modulates gain/alertness and correlates with momentary effort investment.
5. Subjective effort emerges when predicted control costs (dACC) and interoceptive prediction errors (insula) exceed a context-dependent threshold; the resulting signal drives avoidance or compensatory increases in control.

Key testable hypotheses and study designs:
- Hypothesis A (anticipatory): dACC activity and midfrontal theta during cue/anticipation scale with predicted effort cost and predict both subsequent performance and trial-wise subjective effort ratings. Design: task with parametric effort levels (mental load or physical force), trial-by-trial ratings, EEG-fMRI fusion.
- Hypothesis B (interoceptive contribution): anterior insula activity and insula–dACC functional connectivity predict subjective effort over and above objective demand and peripheral arousal (HRV, skin conductance). Design: concurrent fMRI + ECG + SCR + ratings; mediation analysis testing insula as mediator between physiological signals and subjective reports.
- Hypothesis C (motivation modulation): DA manipulation (e.g., methylphenidate or placebo, or dopaminergic agonist/antagonist in animals) changes effort sensitivity in behavior and alters striatum–dACC coupling. Design: pharmacological RCT with effort discounting tasks and model-based fMRI.
- Hypothesis D (temporal dynamics): anticipatory, online, and recovery phases have distinct signatures: anticipatory dACC predicts choice; online insula and pupil reflect momentary subjective effort; post-task ACC/insula activity correlates with fatigue/recovery. Design: long-duration sustained-effort tasks with continuous physiological monitoring.

Methods and analyses to prioritize:
- Model-based fMRI/EEG linking computational variables (expected cost, control signal, prediction errors) to neural timecourses.
- Multimodal measures: fMRI + EEG + pupillometry + autonomic data to dissociate valuation, control, and interoceptive components.
- Causal manipulations: pharmacology, TMS/tDCS to dACC/insula, and cross-species electrophysiology in rodents to measure single-unit/circuit mechanisms.
- Individual differences and clinical extensions: include measures of trait motivation, apathy, depression, and fatigue; test whether altered dACC–insula dynamics underlie clinical changes in effort experience.

Implementation priorities: preregistered, adequately powered within-subject designs, trial-wise subjective ratings, computational modeling, and multimodal acquisition to map neural signatures to the phenomenology of effort.
