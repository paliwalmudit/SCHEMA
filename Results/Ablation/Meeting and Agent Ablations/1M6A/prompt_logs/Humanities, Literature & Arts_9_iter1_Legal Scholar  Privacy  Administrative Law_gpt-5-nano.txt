Japan currently governs non-medical human-subjects research through a patchwork of sectoral guidelines, institutional review practices, funding-agency conditions, and general data-protection law rather than a single coherent statute. This approach preserves institutional flexibility but produces inconsistent review quality, unclear rules for secondary/large-scale data use, and limited normative guidance for novel modalities (social-media research, AI/big data, online experiments).

Principal gaps and inconsistencies
- Lack of unified national guidance specific to non-medical human-subjects research creates variation in scope of review, consent expectations, and risk/benefit assessment across institutions.  
- Ambiguity in how APPI principles apply to research contexts, secondary use, de-identification thresholds, and cross-border data transfers.  
- Inconsistent committee composition, expertise (social-science methods, data science, legal/privacy), and review timelines impede efficient oversight and fairness.  
- Limited proportional-review frameworks: low-risk social/educational studies often face full-board review while high‑risk data-science projects receive insufficient scrutiny.  
- Insufficient, inconsistent guidance for research using publicly available online data, social media, automated scraping, and AI processing.  
- Capacity gaps at smaller institutions and uneven ethics training for researchers and REC members.  
- Weak transparency: ethics decisions and rationales are typically unpublished, limiting precedent-building and public trust.
