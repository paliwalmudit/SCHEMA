Summary framing and mechanisms to target:
Ground the intervention in a dual focus on cognitive processes central to comprehension (vocabulary, syntactic parsing, working memory–supported integration, inferencing, metacognitive monitoring, and schema activation) and motivational mechanisms from self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) and expectancy-value theory (task value and expectancy of success). Texts generated from students’ prompts can increase personal relevance and perceived control (autonomy), adjust difficulty to support competence, and increase value by connecting content to students’ interests—mechanisms likely to produce both immediate engagement and durable gains in comprehension.

Primary outcome constructs and recommended measures:
- Reading comprehension: assess literal, inferential, and integrative comprehension with standardized and curriculum-aligned measures (e.g., Woodcock-Johnson Passage Comprehension or Gates-MacGinitie Reading Tests for group-level outcomes) plus researcher-developed question sets targeting inferencing and integration. Include cloze or retell tasks to capture integration and recall.
- Reading motivation and engagement: use validated scales such as the Motivation for Reading Questionnaire (MRQ; Wigfield & Guthrie) for intrinsic/extrinsic dimensions, a Reading Self-Efficacy scale, and the Student Engagement Instrument (SEI) for behavioral/emotional engagement. Supplement with reading choice measures and logs (frequency/duration).
- Process measures: comprehension strategy use via think-alouds or self-report strategy inventories, metacognitive monitoring accuracy (judgments of learning vs. actual performance), and time-on-task/interaction logs with texts.

Intervention dosage and scaffolding:
Recommend 8–12 weeks, 2–3 sessions/week, 20–30 minutes/session embedded in regular reading instruction. Structure progression: (1) pre-reading scaffolds (prompt refinement, goal-setting), (2) AI-generated personalized text reading with scaffolds (pre-teach key vocabulary, guided questions), (3) active processing tasks (summaries, self-explanation, question-generation), and (4) fading scaffolds toward independent reading and reflection. Provide adaptive difficulty (lexile or readability tuning) and explicit strategy prompts early, fading to learner-generated prompts.

Design, mediators, moderators, and analysis suggestions:
Use randomized or matched control conditions (teacher-selected texts, non-personalized AI texts) and collect fidelity data. Test mediation models where increases in autonomy/ perceived value and in specific cognitive skills (vocabulary, inferencing, monitoring) mediate comprehension and motivation outcomes. Examine moderators: baseline reading skill, age, SES, and digital literacy. Analyze using multilevel models/latent growth models and formal mediation analyses.

Practical and ethical considerations:
Include human review of AI outputs for accuracy and bias, train teachers on prompt design, and ensure equitable access. Collect qualitative feedback from students and teachers to interpret motivational changes and to guide iterative improvements.

These elements will tightly link theory, measurement, and practice to determine how student-prompted AI-generated texts influence both the cognitive processes of comprehension and the motivational levers that sustain reading engagement.
