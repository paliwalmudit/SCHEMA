Working definition (working): Social cohesion in African contexts is the dynamic degree of connectedness, reciprocity and legitimacy that binds diverse people into functioning, resilient collectives capable of peaceful cooperation and equitable development. It emerges from individual attitudes, intergroup relations, and institutional arrangements shaped by historical, economic and cultural contexts.

Conceptual framework (summary): Social cohesion is a multi-level, multi-dimensional process with three interacting domains:
- Relational (micro & meso): patterns of trust, social networks, reciprocity and intergroup contact that enable cooperation within and across groups.
- Normative-cognitive (attitudinal): shared norms, sense of belonging, civic identity, tolerance, and perceived fairness that orient behaviour toward collective goals.
- Institutional-structural (macro): state legitimacy, inclusive institutions, equitable resource distribution, rule of law, and customary practices that structure opportunities and constraints.

Cross-cutting dimensions important in African contexts:
- Horizontal equity and recognition: reduction of group-based inequalities (ethnic, regional, colonial-era dispossession), and recognition of customary identities and institutions.
- Inclusion and representation: political and economic inclusion of marginalized groups, women, youth and migrants.
- Legitimacy and procedural fairness: perceived neutrality and effectiveness of security, justice and public service institutions.
- Resilience to conflict: capacities for non-violent dispute resolution, social insurance mechanisms, and bridging ties that prevent escalation.

How components interact: Individual attitudes (trust, belonging) are shaped by everyday interactions and experiences with institutions; institutional fairness and redistribution influence material grievances that drive intergroup tensions; bridging networks and shared norms moderate how conflicts are mobilized. Feedback loops mean interventions must address attitudes, structures and interactions simultaneously.

Measurement implications and candidate indicators:
- Multi-level approach: household surveys (perceptions), behavioural measures, administrative data, community mapping, and qualitative narratives.
- Relational indicators: generalized trust, interpersonal trust across groups, density of cross-cutting social ties, frequency/quality of intergroup contact, civic cooperation rates.
- Normative-cognitive indicators: sense of national/local belonging, tolerance scales, willingness to engage in cross-group associations, perceived discrimination.
- Institutional indicators: trust in institutions, perceived impartiality of justice/security, representation indices, access to services, grievance redress effectiveness.
- Structural/economic indicators: group income gaps, land tenure security, service inequality, spatial segregation indices.
- Conflict/resilience indicators: incidence of local disputes, presence of functioning mediation mechanisms, rapid social recovery after shocks.

Measurement principles: prioritize mixed methods, longitudinal and spatially disaggregated data, adapt instruments to local languages and institutions, and balance universal comparability with context sensitivity (customary authorities, ethnic logics, migration dynamics).

It emerges from individual attitudes, intergroup relations, and institutional arrangements shaped by historical, economic and cultural contexts.

Conceptual framework (summary): Social cohesion is a multi-level, multi-dimensional process with three interacting domains:
- Relational (micro & meso): patterns of trust, social networks, reciprocity and intergroup contact that enable cooperation within and across groups.
- Normative-cognitive (attitudinal): shared norms, sense of belonging, civic identity, tolerance, and perceived fairness that orient behaviour toward collective goals.
- Institutional-structural (macro): state legitimacy, inclusive institutions, equitable resource distribution, rule of law, and customary practices that structure opportunities and constraints.

Cross-cutting dimensions important in African contexts:
- Horizontal equity and recognition: reduction of group-based inequalities (ethnic, regional, colonial-era dispossession), and recognition of customary identities and institutions.
- Inclusion and representation: political and economic inclusion of marginalized groups, women, youth and migrants.
- Legitimacy and procedural fairness: perceived neutrality and effectiveness of security, justice and public service institutions.
- Resilience to conflict: capacities for non-violent dispute resolution, social insurance mechanisms, and bridging ties that prevent escalation.

How components interact: Individual attitudes (trust, belonging) are shaped by everyday interactions and experiences with institutions; institutional fairness and redistribution influence material grievances that drive intergroup tensions; bridging networks and shared norms moderate how conflicts are mobilized. Feedback loops mean interventions must address attitudes, structures and interactions simultaneously.

Measurement implications and candidate indicators:
- Multi-level approach: household surveys (perceptions), behavioural measures, administrative data, community mapping, and qualitative narratives.
- Relational indicators: generalized trust, interpersonal trust across groups, density of cross-cutting social ties, frequency/quality of intergroup contact, civic cooperation rates.
- Normative-cognitive indicators: sense of national/local belonging, tolerance scales, willingness to engage in cross-group associations, perceived discrimination.
- Institutional indicators: trust in institutions, perceived impartiality of justice/security, representation indices, access to services, grievance redress effectiveness.
- Structural/economic indicators: group income gaps, land tenure security, service inequality, spatial segregation indices.
- Conflict/resilience indicators: incidence of local disputes, presence of functioning mediation mechanisms, rapid social recovery after shocks.

Measurement principles: prioritize mixed methods, longitudinal and spatially disaggregated data, adapt instruments to local languages and institutions, and balance universal comparability with context sensitivity (customary authorities, ethnic logics, migration dynamics).
