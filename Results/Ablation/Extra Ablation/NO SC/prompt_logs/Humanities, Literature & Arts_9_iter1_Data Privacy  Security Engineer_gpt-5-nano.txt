4. Digital/social-media ambiguity: publicly visible posts may still be experienced as private; scraping and quoting can cause harm.  
5. Vulnerable and hierarchical contexts: children, elderly, employees, and marginalized groups face coercion or disproportionate harms.  
6. Emotional/psychological risks in interviews/experiments: sensitive topics can trigger distress or social repercussions.

Risk-tiering criteria and review standards
- Low risk (expedited/exempt): anonymous surveys on non-sensitive topics, observational studies in truly public spaces with no recording of identifiers. Require basic privacy safeguards and a brief protocol summary.  
- Medium risk (expedited/full review): identifiable survey data, focus groups, interviews on moderately sensitive topics, non-invasive behavioral experiments, or studies involving gatekeepers. Require detailed consent plan, data minimization, and storage plan.  
- High risk (full review + DPIA and monitoring): research with sensitive topics (trauma, illegal behavior), vulnerable populations, collection of biosocial or precise geolocation data, large-scale social-media scraping with verbatim quotes, or secondary use of identifiable administrative data. Require full ERC review, data protection impact assessment, and justification for any waiver of consent.

Mitigation strategies for researchers
1. Consent: use layered, culturally appropriate consent (written when appropriate; oral/group briefings and witnessed consent when culturally preferable); document gatekeeper interactions and demonstrate individual voluntariness.  
2. Data minimization and de-identification: collect only necessary variables, apply pseudonymization, assess re-identification risk, and avoid verbatim quotes that reveal context; use delayed or aggregated reporting for small communities.  
3. Digital ethics: justify scraping, prefer paraphrase over verbatim quotes, consider account-level consent for private or semi-private groups, and avoid cross-linking datasets that increase identifiability.  
4. Governance: include data flow diagrams, retention schedules, encryption, access controls, and cross-border transfer safeguards; obtain DPAs where subcontracting.  
5. Participant protection: protocolize distress management, referral pathways, and opt-out routes; avoid undue inducement.  
6. Community engagement: conduct pre-research consultations, use advisory panels or community liaisons, and provide study summaries in accessible language.

Operational recommendations for ERCs and researchers
- Require concise risk matrix and DPIA for medium/high-risk studies, and standardized templates for gatekeeper plans and consent justifications.  
- Ensure ERC composition includes social-methods experts and community representatives and provide case-based training on qualitative and digital methods.  
- Fund empirical audits: track ERC decisions, researcher experiences, and participant preferences to iteratively refine tiers and templates.

These measures balance participant protection, cultural responsiveness, and research feasibility, enabling proportionate, consistent ethical review for non-medical research in Japan."
