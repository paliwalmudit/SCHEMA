This framework clarifies why quantification remains challenging and points to operational measures for empirical testing.

Core definitions: A module is a cohesive unit that performs a set of identifiable functions and interacts with other units only through specified interfaces. Modularity is an architectural property defined by three orthogonal dimensions: decomposability (the partitioning of a system into modules), interface specification (degree and quality of formalized interaction rules), and module granularity (size and functional scope of modules). Organizational modularity denotes alignment of team boundaries and governance with architectural modules.

Reconciliation of competing definitions: Structural modularity (component partitioning), functional modularity (encapsulation of functions), and organizational modularity (teams and suppliers mapped to modules) are complementary perspectives on the same underlying decomposition. Disagreement in the literature often arises from conflating dimension emphasis; my framework treats them as measurable dimensions that interact causally.

Causal pathways and hypotheses: Modularity affects product-family outcomes through two primary pathways. Design-time pathway: higher decomposability and clear interfaces enable parallel development, reuse, and reduced variant-specific design effort, which should decrease development lead time and marginal cost per variant. Runtime pathway: well-specified interfaces and sufficient granularity enable substitution and upgrades, increasing product variety and upgradeability but potentially increasing system integration overhead.

Representative testable hypotheses:
H1: Higher interface specification quality increases reuse rate, which reduces marginal development cost per variant.
H2: Greater decomposability (more modules with low coupling) increases parallel development capacity and reduces average time-to-market per variant, conditional on organizational modularity.
H3: Finer granularity increases achievable product-family variety but raises integration testing effort, producing a U-shaped relationship with total development cost.
H4: The positive effects of modularity on variant cost reduction are moderated negatively by high technological turbulence and positively by platform governance maturity.

This framework clarifies why quantification remains challenging and points to operational measures for empirical testing.

Core definitions: A module is a cohesive unit that performs a set of identifiable functions and interacts with other units only through specified interfaces. Modularity is an architectural property defined by three orthogonal dimensions: decomposability (the partitioning of a system into modules), interface specification (degree and quality of formalized interaction rules), and module granularity (size and functional scope of modules). Organizational modularity denotes alignment of team boundaries and governance with architectural modules.

Reconciliation of competing definitions: Structural modularity (component partitioning), functional modularity (encapsulation of functions), and organizational modularity (teams and suppliers mapped to modules) are complementary perspectives on the same underlying decomposition. Disagreement in the literature often arises from conflating dimension emphasis; my framework treats them as measurable dimensions that interact causally.

Causal pathways and hypotheses: Modularity affects product-family outcomes through two primary pathways. Design-time pathway: higher decomposability and clear interfaces enable parallel development, reuse, and reduced variant-specific design effort, which should decrease development lead time and marginal cost per variant. Runtime pathway: well-specified interfaces and sufficient granularity enable substitution and upgrades, increasing product variety and upgradeability but potentially increasing system integration overhead.

Representative testable hypotheses:
H1: Higher interface specification quality increases reuse rate, which reduces marginal development cost per variant.
H2: Greater decomposability (more modules with low coupling) increases parallel development capacity and reduces average time-to-market per variant, conditional on organizational modularity.
H3: Finer granularity increases achievable product-family variety but raises integration testing effort, producing a U-shaped relationship with total development cost.
H4: The positive effects of modularity on variant cost reduction are moderated negatively by high technological turbulence and positively by platform governance maturity.

Operational measures: Decomposability measured by module count, distribution of module sizes (lines of code, parts per module), and coupling metrics (#interfaces, information flow intensity). Interface specification quality measured by presence and completeness of interface documents, formal API/connector standards, and conformance rates. Granularity measured by variance and entropy of module sizes. Reuse rate measured as proportion of modules shared across variants. Outcomes measured as number of variants, time-to-market, marginal cost per variant, warranty/field-failure propagation rate.

Data sources and methods: Use BOM/CAD/ECAD, version-control and PLM logs, API documentation repositories, time sheets, supplier contracts, and failure databases. Combine quantitative network/graph metrics with qualitative assessments (governance maturity scores).

Research gaps: Need for longitudinal data linking architecture evolution to firm outcomes, standardized measurement protocols for interface quality, and causal tests of interaction effects (architecture × organization × market turbulence). This framework provides the constructs and hypotheses to guide such empirical work."
