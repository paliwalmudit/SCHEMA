Proposed definition
Social cohesion in African contexts is a dynamic and multilayered state of societal connectedness characterized by (1) a felt togetherness and mutual belonging across multiple overlapping identities; (2) sustained interpersonal and intergroup trust and reciprocity; (3) shared and legitimate institutions and norms that mediate conflict and allocate resources fairly; and (4) active forms of civic and communal engagement that enable collective action. This definition foregrounds both subjective experience (belonging, recognition) and objective structures (institutions, resource distribution), and it explicitly incorporates African epistemic notions of relationality (e.g., ubuntu, communal reciprocity) and customary governance alongside formal institutions.

Core dimensions and brief operational logic
- Belonging and recognition: subjective sense of being accepted within community/society and recognition of multiple identities. Measured by self-reports, narrative accounts, and indicators of symbolic inclusion.
- Relational trust and reciprocity: generalized and cross-group trust, expectations of mutual aid, and functioning informal networks. Measured via trust scales, reciprocity vignettes, and social network density.
- Institutional legitimacy and fairness: perceived legitimacy of formal and customary institutions, rule compliance, and procedural fairness in dispute resolution and resource allocation. Measured by confidence in institutions, grievance resolution rates, and perceptions of impartiality.
- Equity and inclusion: distributional justice across identities; access to land, services, and political voice. Measured by objective inequality metrics, perceived fairness, and representation indices.
- Civic engagement and collective action: participation in communal activities, intergroup collaboration, and voluntary association. Measured by participation rates, joint projects, and willingness to cooperate across lines.

- Inclusive institutions (formal + customary) that mediate differences increase trust and reduce grievance-driven fragmentation.
