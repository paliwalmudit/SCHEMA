Theoretical framing
Populist logic centers on a people-versus-elite binary, claims of moral homogeneity of “the people,” constructions of crisis, and appeals to popular sovereignty. When transposed into corporate strategy, these logics enable firms to reframe consumption as political belonging: businesses portray themselves or their brands as defenders of “ordinary consumers” against out‑of‑touch elites (regulators, experts, competitors), mobilize customers through identity appeals, and legitimate unconventional market practices (bypassing intermediaries, resisting regulation) as popular will. Corporate populism thus operates both rhetorically (framing) and strategically (positioning, mobilization, regulatory signaling), producing hybrid subjectivities (consumer-citizen) and altering political contestation around markets.

Taxonomy of populist rhetoric for business communications (operational categories with short indicators)
1. People-centrism: explicit appeals to “ordinary people,” “real customers,” “everyday families.” Indicators: first-person plural, imagery of common life.
2. Anti-elite antagonism: denigration of elites (politicians, regulators, experts, incumbent firms). Indicators: contrastive language, blame attribution.
3. Crisis/threat framing: framing market/regulatory environment as urgent threat to consumers. Indicators: urgency markers, disaster metaphors.
4. Moral homogeneity: claims that customers share common values and are morally superior to outsiders. Indicators: moralizing language, purity/decency appeals.
5. Boundary/nativist exclusion: privileging natives/local consumers or national identity. Indicators: “our” nation/language/culture as selling point.
6. Anti-expertise/epistemic populism: distrust of experts, elevation of common-sense knowledge. Indicators: dismissal of studies, “we know better” tropes.
7. Leader personalization/charismatic CEO framing: CEO as champion of the people. Indicators: CEO-as-savior language, direct address.
8. Economic redistribution/consumer championing: promises of fairness, lower prices, reclaiming value for consumers. Indicators: fairness rhetoric, blame on intermediaries.

Qualitative coding schema (practical rules)
- Unit of analysis: discrete communications (ads, press releases, CEO letters, tweets, campaign pages, CSR reports).
